Why the page was convincing

  • Cloned UI — same logo, colours, fonts and layout as the real service.
  • Familiar flow — "Forgot password?", "Save login info" and the back button all behave like the real thing.
  • Hidden anti-bot markers — the form carries a hidden field and an X-Requested-With-style header so naive crawlers are skipped (real kits do this too).
  • Redirection — real kits bounce you to the genuine site afterwards, so you never notice you leaked your credentials.

How to spot it next time

  • Check the URL bar. The domain is the single best signal. Lookalike domains (e.g. snаpchat.com with a Cyrillic а) are the #1 trick — the letters look identical.
  • A padlock does not mean the site is legit — phishing pages use HTTPS too.
  • Never reuse passwords. If you typed one here, treat it as compromised: change it, and enable two-factor authentication on the real service.
  • When in doubt, type the address yourself instead of clicking links.

About this demo

Notice this page had no warning when you opened it — that is the point. Real phishing pages never announce themselves; the disclosure only comes now, after you already typed your credentials. In this online demo the whole flow runs in your own browser: nothing was sent to any server, nothing was written to disk, and the values above were wiped the moment this page rendered. (The classroom version of this demo runs locally on 127.0.0.1 and logs submissions to captured_credentials.txt so the instructor can show the class the "attacker's file".) In a real attack those credentials would be used against your real accounts — which is exactly why you should never reuse passwords, and why you should change anything you typed here.
